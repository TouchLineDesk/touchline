Season 2 · eight games in

Two leagues, one league

The Sixteen are playing two competitions at once and the results do not look like they belong to the same teams. In their own league they are grinding: six clubs inside two points, Michigan and North Carolina tied at the top, Florida last of the sixteen on four points from seven. In conference play against the rest of college soccer they have played forty, won forty, and scored three hundred and thirty-six goals while conceding nine. Florida, bottom of one, are four from four with a goal difference of thirty-nine in the other. Both of those things are true about the same football team.

What this is. Eight matchdays for some, six for others — the schedule is uneven and everything below is rate-based where it can be. Read from the save this morning: the full league table, every appearance and shot and tackle in the Sixteen, three conference competitions, the Youth League group stage, the national team, and all sixty-seven graduates in their first professional season. New this edition: Touchline Grades. The method is set out where they start, and the short version is that they measure what a player did, never what the game says he is.

New · a permanent fixture

Touchline Grades

Every one of the sixteen clubs plays the same eleven roles. That is unusual, and it makes something possible that most leagues cannot do honestly: a left winger at LSU and a left winger at Michigan have the same job description, so comparing them is a fair comparison rather than a fudge. From this edition on, every player carries a grade out of one hundred, scored against the other players doing his exact job.

The grade measures what a player did, not what the game says he is. Current ability never enters the calculation. That is deliberate: this desk already publishes ability rankings, and a grade built on ability would only be ability again. Keeping them apart is what lets us ask the question worth asking — who is outplaying his talent, and who is not.

Each role is scored on the things that role is for. A ball-playing defender is graded on tackles won as a share of tackles made, interceptions, aerial duels, passing accuracy and volume, and is punished for mistakes leading to goals. An advanced forward is graded on goals, shot accuracy, and how many he scored against the number an average finisher would have scored from his chances. A sweeper keeper is graded mostly on saves against expected saves, because clean sheets are mostly the eleven players in front of him. Average match rating is in every role at roughly a quarter of the weight — enough to catch what the counting stats miss, not enough to be the whole grade.

Short samples are pulled toward the middle rather than to the bottom. Eight games is not many, and a player with three appearances and a flattering rating is not evidence of anything, so the composite is shrunk in proportion to minutes played. Nobody tops a leaderboard on a cameo.

The scale is set within the season. Sixty-one is average, the eighties are excellent, and only two players are above ninety. We calibrate each season separately on purpose: in a competition where the entire population gets better every year, an absolute scale would drift until everyone graded ninety. This way, eighty-five always means one of the best in the league that year.

What it is not. It is not a transfer valuation, it does not know about tactics or instructions, and it cannot see a defender who was in the right place all afternoon and therefore made no tackles. Eight matches is a small sample and these numbers will move. We are publishing them anyway, with the minutes shown next to every one, because a grade you can argue with is more useful than a ranking nobody can check.

Rumblings · a recurring item

The Garrett file, No. 2

Last month this desk printed four things it could not stand up about a fourteen-year-old in Livonia, Michigan. One of them has moved. We now know what Daniel Garrett plays, and it is not what anybody guessed.

He is a wing back. Specifically he is the thing this competition has built its entire shape around and has almost none of: an inverted wing back who can play either flank at the same level. Three separate people have now described the same player to us — left or right, no drop-off, comfortable inside, and quick enough that the position does not contain him.

That matters more here than it would anywhere else. All sixteen clubs play two inverted wing backs on attack duty. It is the only role in the shape that is duplicated on opposite flanks, which means a player who covers both is worth two squad places instead of one — and the Academy is capped at twenty-two. The grades in this edition make the scarcity concrete: of sixty-one wing backs who have played enough to qualify, exactly two are above eighty-five, and the best of them are specialists.

What has not moved is everything else. We still have not seen him play. He is still committed to nobody. Nobody will name the clubs who have watched him, and the one scout who has been most forthcoming about the position went quiet the moment he was asked who else knew.

The frame from last month stands. In 2004 an American fourteen-year-old was the most famous young footballer in the world and it did not work out, and the base rate on rumours like this one is brutal. But the position is a real detail, it is the first hard thing we have had, and it points at the one hole every squad in this league is carrying.

Sixty-seven rookies, three hundred and one starts

The Class of 2022, a quarter into year one

The Class of 2022 is a quarter of the way through its first professional season. Between them the sixty-seven have made three hundred and one starts and earned sixty-five million dollars. The median graduate has started five matches. Eleven have not started one.

The top of the draft has held up. Tyler Adams, third overall, leads the class with twelve starts at Minnesota and an average rating of 7.34. Mark McKenzie, tenth, also has twelve. Christian Pulisic has the best rating of any regular at 7.88 from eight starts at LAFC, on a salary of seven point two million — the largest in the class by fifty thousand dollars over Weston McKennie.

The bottom has not. Eleven graduates have not started a professional match, and the names are not all late picks: Marlon Fossey went thirteenth and is at TCU, back in college soccer.

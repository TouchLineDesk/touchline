Special edition · the 2023 Under-20 World Cup

Favourites. For once.

The United States have never finished in the top three of this tournament. Not once, in forty-six years of it. They go to Indonesia tomorrow with twenty-one players, every one of them from the Sixteen, and a best eleven rated thirteen points above the next best squad in the field. Touchline read all twenty-four squads from the save this morning and played the tournament twenty thousand times. The U.S.A. won it 63% of the time. Nobody else won it one time in seven.

By the Touchline desk · squads read from the save on Wednesday, May 24, 2023, the day before kick-off

What this edition is. An Under-20 World Cup issue and nothing else. The twenty-one and where each of them plays his college football; Touchline’s starting eleven in the shape the program plays, a back four, one holding midfielder, three behind two strikers; Group D game by game; how far the U.S.A. should go and what would have to happen for them to go out; all twenty-four squads rated; the favourites, the dark horses and the teams that have no chance; the players worth the ticket; our bracket to June 17; and the seven Academy players wearing somebody else’s shirt.

How the numbers work. Every squad was read from the save today. A team’s strength is the average ability of its best eleven, one goalkeeper and the ten best outfield players. We played every group game and every tie twenty thousand times with the second round drawn the way this tournament draws it: the six group winners, the six runners-up and the four best third-placed teams. The percentages are those twenty thousand tournaments. They know nothing about tactics, form, the heat in Jakarta or how a 16-year-old handles a penalty shoot-out, which is to say: useful, and wrong often enough to make the thing worth watching.

Second striker: Johnson. The model says Oliva (123) or Clark (124). The case for Garrett. Daniel Garrett is 15, rated 126, two points behind Greene at right back, with a 7.69 average from ten Michigan games and a ceiling the save puts at 200, the highest of any player at the tournament. The desk has him on the bench, and as the first change. Nobody in this squad has more riding on June than he does: a 15-year-old who starts a World Cup knockout tie is the kind of thing the Almanac keeps a page for.

South Korea, Colombia, Spain · the U.S.A. open on Sunday, May 28

Group D has the strongest team in the tournament and the third-strongest, which makes it the only group with a real game in it. The U.S.A. win the group 83% of the time, finish second 16%, and average 8.0 points, 10.5 goals scored and 1.7 conceded across the three games.

Why first matters

The second round pairs the Group D winner with a third-placed team from B, E or F: Iran, Tunisia, Mexico, South Africa or New Zealand, most of the time. The Group D runner-up gets the winner of Group E, which is England two times in three and Argentina most of the rest. Finishing first is worth about eleven points of title probability in our model, almost all of it from not meeting England before the final.

How far should they go?

The path, round by round

All the way. The model has the U.S.A. in the final 77% of the time and lifting it 63%. The most likely way they go out is a semi-final, usually against Brazil; the round of 16 and the quarter-final put together account for fewer than one tournament in ten.

Who they meet

Round of 16: Iran 24%, Mexico 15%, England 14%, Argentina 12%, Tunisia 11%. As group winners the opponent is a third-placed team, and the model’s most common one is Iran, who come third in Group B behind Serbia and Tunisia.

Quarter-final: Ivory Coast 47%, Portugal 16%, Canada 10.0%, Brazil 8.1%. The tie feeds from the Group A runner-up against the Group C runner-up, which is why an African side rated 85 is the most likely quarter-final opponent for the favourites.

Semi-final: Brazil 58%, Argentina 7.4%, Mexico 4.6%, England 4.5%. Brazil win Group A 90% of the time and their half of the bracket has no one else in it; the semi-final is the one game before the final the U.S.A. are not very heavy favourites to win.

Final: England 28%, Spain 20%, Argentina 12%, Portugal 9.7%. One tie at a time, the model gives the U.S.A. 81% against Brazil, 80% against Spain and 75% against England, extra time and penalties included.

Three versions of the next three weeks

The expected version · about five in eight
Champions

Win the group, beat Brazil, win the final

Top Group D, which happens 83% of the time. A third-placed team in the round of 16, the Group A or C runner-up in the quarter-final, Brazil in the semi-final, and England or Spain on June 17. In this version the U.S.A. win something they have never been within a game of.

The honourable version · about one in four
Top three

Lose the semi-final or the final

A first top-three finish would still be the best Under-20 result in the country’s history. A semi-final defeat and a third-place win happens 12% of the time; a lost final 14%. Either way, a medal, which there has never been.

The bad version · about one in eleven
Out before the semis

Second in the group, England in the round of 16

Lose to Spain, finish second, and the bracket turns: England in the round of 16 (75% to win it, not 99%), Brazil a round earlier. The U.S.A. go out before the semi-final in 9.2% of tournaments, and most of those begin with the Spain game.

The Academy without them

The league does not stop. The USLA’s final round is Saturday, May 27, the day before the U.S.A. open against South Korea, and the twenty-one are already in Indonesia. Texas go to Miami without five players, among them Okoh, Gómez and the two Texas strikers, Cowell and George Johnson; Michigan (4) and Alabama (4) are the next hardest hit, and Georgia lose Leyva and Henry. Who sends how many: Texas 5, Alabama 4, Michigan 4, Georgia 2, Florida State 1, Miami 1, North Carolina 1, Ohio State 1, Oklahoma 1, Washington 1.

Never in the top three

What the U.S.A. are playing for

The tournament has been played since 1977 and the United States have never finished in its top three. A fourth place, in 1989, is the high-water mark; since then, quarter-finals at best. Argentina have won it six times, Brazil five, and of the twenty-four teams here, England, Spain, Portugal and Serbia have also lifted it. Ukraine, the holders, did not qualify.

So the question Touchline was asked is the right one: is this the year of something great? The honest answer from a model that only knows ability ratings is that it had better be. The U.S.A. have never been favourites for this tournament. They have never been in the top four of the field on ability, let alone first by thirteen points. A squad this good at this age is what a league like the Sixteen was supposed to produce, and the Academy’s second season has produced it: twenty-one players, twelve of them rated 125 or better, every one of them playing week-in, week-out in a competitive league since August, against each other.

What the model cannot see is the thing that usually decides tournaments like this: one bad half against Spain, a 16-year-old at the end of a long season, a knockout tie in 90-degree heat decided from twelve yards. The desk’s call is the model’s call, with the caveat printed in large type: a 63% favourite loses more than one time in three. The U.S.A. should win this tournament. They are also, at the same odds, more likely to leave without it than any one other team is to win it.

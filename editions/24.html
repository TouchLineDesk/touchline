Special edition No. 2 · the 2023 Under-20 World Cup · final whistle

Champions of the world.

Forty-six years, no top-three finish, a fourth place in 1989 as the best of it. Then seven games in Indonesia, seven wins, and a final won 6–0. The United States are Under-20 world champions, with a squad that came, every one of the twenty-one, from the Sixteen.

By the Touchline desk · results, scorers and abilities read from the save after the final on Saturday, June 17, 2023

What this edition is. The wrap-up of the tournament we previewed on May 24, and nothing else. The final and the road to it, game by game; every one of the twenty-one, with a mark and a paragraph; what the players, the head coach and our panel said; how the desk’s calls held up; the full bracket; the seven Academy players who wore somebody else’s shirt; what comes next, for the Under-20s, the Olympic cycle and the senior team; and the question we were asked to answer straight: what this says about the Academy’s ability to raise world-class players.

What the save can and cannot tell us. It keeps every result and every goal, with the scorer and the minute. It does not keep, in any form we can read, who started a national-team game, who came on, or how anybody was rated. So the player marks below are built from what is visible: goals, the results of the games, the defensive record, ability before and after, and what each player was in May. One game, the round of 16 against England, could not be read at all; we know who won it and nothing else, and we say so wherever it matters.

The final

Saturday, June 17 · 20:00 · U.S.A. 6–0 Portugal
Under-20 World Cup final · Indonesia 2023

It was over in five minutes, in the sense that matters. Moses Nyeman, a holding midfielder with seven goals in a whole college season, scored the first. By half-time it was four.

Portugal were not a soft final opponent. They had won Group C, put five past New Zealand in the round of 16, beaten Canada 1–0 in the quarter-final and Colombia 4–2 in the semi, with Joelson Fernandes and Rodrigo Ribeiro scoring two each. The model gave them a 2.2% chance of the title on May 24 and they spent three weeks making that number look stingy. Then they met a team whose best eleven was rated about twenty-six points higher than theirs, and the number looked about right after all.

Cade Cowell made it two on 26 minutes, his fourth of the tournament. Ricardo Pepi, who had not scored until the semi-final, scored for the third time in two games on 36. George Johnson made it four before the break. Andrey Yakovlev, a 17-year-old centre-back from Washington, made it five five minutes after it, and Johnson scored again a minute later.

That was 6–0 on 51 minutes, and for the last thirty-nine minutes of an Under-20 World Cup final nothing happened at all. No Portugal goal; the U.S. had conceded none in the semi-final and none here, and three in the six games the save lets us count.

The scorers say something about the team. A holding midfielder, two strikers, a winger-forward, a centre-back. Six goals from five players, three of them from Texas. And the youngest two scorers were sixteen and seventeen.

The road

Six games before the final · scores written U.S. goals first

Three group games, nine points. A round of 16 against the team everybody expected in the final. Then South Africa, Spain for the second time, and Portugal. The U.S. scored in every game, and in the games we can read, trailed in none.

The mixed zone

Jess Ocampo, Touchline Live, after the final

Jess Ocampo used to call Academy-age games from behind a dugout with one camera and a thermos. On Saturday night she was behind a barrier in Indonesia with a microphone and about forty other people, and she still got to everybody first.

The head coach came through last, as head coaches do, and was asked the obvious thing first.

“Forty-six years. I was told that about four hundred times before we left. I didn’t tell the players. They read it anyway. I don’t think it weighed anything tonight. The first goal took the weight off, and after that it was a final we were allowed to enjoy, which I didn’t expect and I won’t pretend I did.” — the U.S. head coach

Then the question Walt Kearney has been asking in print since May: twenty-one players from ten of the Sixteen, and none from the school the coach runs.

“I said last year I know my own players too well to give them the benefit of the doubt. I picked twenty-one I didn’t have to be fair to. I’d pick the same twenty-one tomorrow. Walt can keep writing it. Tonight he can write it with a medal in the picture.” — the U.S. head coach

The honest version

The big call was right. Almost everything about the route was wrong: the model expected England and Brazil to be the problems, and neither reached the semi-finals. The U.S.A. went through the side of the draw that had England on it and beat them in the round of 16. A model that only knows ability ratings is good at picking the best team and bad at picking everybody else’s tournament, and that is roughly what it said about itself in May.

What’s next

The Under-20s, the Olympic cycle, the senior team

Tuesday: back to school, all of them. Draft 23 opens in Seattle on June 20 and none of the twenty-one is in it. Every champion is back in the Sixteen in August, which means the league starts next season with a world title spread across ten of its programs.

The 2025 Under-20 World Cup. Only players born on or after January 1, 2005 are eligible, and four of the twenty-one qualify: Daniel Garrett, George Johnson, Andrey Yakovlev and Eli Bradford. Seventeen of the champions have played their last Under-20 game. The defence of the title will be built around a sixteen-year-old striker with a 180 ceiling and a fifteen-year-old wing back with a 200 one, and behind them is the deepest crop of American newgens the Academy has had (table). This is the first time a U.S. Under-20 team will go into a cycle as holders. Nobody should expect a 63% favourite again; a team with Johnson and Garrett in it is still a team people will be nervous to draw.

The Olympic cycle. The men’s Olympic tournament is an under-23 competition, and all twenty-one are young enough for the next one, if the U.S. qualify. This is where the class of 2003 goes next: Okoh, Pepi, Greene, Gómez, Leyva, Paredes, Clark, Gutiérrez, Che, Nyeman and Cowell. An Olympic squad built on this group, with the older Academy graduates who are now in the USLP, would start as one of the strongest the country has sent.

The senior team. The 2026 World Cup is in North America, three summers away. By then Pepi will be 23, Okoh 23, Henry 22, Słonina 22, Rotundo 21, Johnson 19, turning 20 during the tournament, and Garrett 18. Ability is what gets a player into a senior squad in this game, and the senior bar is high: none of the twenty-one is at 150 yet. The ones with the ceilings to get there are Garrett (200), Johnson (180), Pepi (159), Słonina (155) and Okoh (150). Kearney counted fourteen Academy players in the senior squad in November; this edition did not read the senior squad, so the next count will come when the senior team next meets.

What the title changes is the argument. Until Saturday the Academy’s case to the national team was ability on a screen. Now it is a world title, won by players who will be 18 to 23 at a home World Cup.

The Academy question

Can it raise world-class players? What the title proves, and what it doesn’t

The short answer: the Academy has proved it can gather, hold and play together a generation good enough to win a world title by six goals. It has not yet proved it makes players better than they would have become anyway. The proof of that is coming, and it has a name.

What it proves. Before a ball was kicked, the U.S. best eleven was rated thirteen points above the next team in the field. That gap is the Academy. Every one of the twenty-one had played a full season in a competitive league against the others, rather than scattered across reserve teams, and 49 American players on Academy books are rated 120 or better, which is deeper than most of the nations in this tournament could pick from. The title is the result a system like this is built to produce, and it produced it at the first time of asking.

What it does not prove. Twenty of the twenty-one have been in the Academy since it opened in August 2021. Between them they have gained 248 points of ability. Players of the same ages elsewhere in the Academy gained, on average, a combined 253 over the same period. In other words, the champions developed at almost exactly the league’s normal rate: no faster. The title was won by talent the Academy recruited and kept, growing at the pace the league grows everybody. Whether that pace beats what the same players would have done elsewhere is a question this edition cannot answer, because the save gives us nobody outside the Academy to compare them with on the same terms.

The real test is the newgens. The game invents players as it goes, and the ones it has put into the Academy’s youth system are the only players whose whole development the Academy owns. Four of them are world champions. George Johnson has gone from 98 to 121, two points ahead of the typical rise for his age, and has a ceiling of 180. Daniel Garrett, 15, is at 128 with a ceiling of 200. Those two are the case for the Academy. Andrey Yakovlev, four points of growth when his age group averages twenty-two, and Eli Bradford, fifteen against twenty-two, are the case against.

The verdict. “World class” in this game means a player rated 150 or more, and none of the twenty-one is there yet. The Academy has shown it can produce a world-champion team. Whether it can produce a world-class player will be settled by Johnson and Garrett over the next three seasons, and if either one reaches 170, that will be the clearest answer the desk could ask for.
